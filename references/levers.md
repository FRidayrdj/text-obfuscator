# Structural rules and levers (R1–R36, R42)

Source: StoryScope (Russell, Rajendhran, Pham, Iyyer & Wieting), a study comparing AI-written and human-written stories. This file gives the *direction* of each finding (humans do more or less of X than AI) and leaves out the figures, which were not re-checked against the paper. The guidance works without them.

## How to read an entry

- **Evidence**: the direction of the human/AI difference the paper found. Human fiction is a useful ceiling or floor, not a target to hit exactly.
- **Do**: the per-piece behavior. Everything is phrased as a decision about *this* piece, not a rate across pieces.
- **Skip when**: cases where the guideline would hurt the work.

**A caution that applies to the whole file.** These describe how AI and human fiction *differ*. Matching the human average is not the same as writing well. Several entries (especially R7) will sometimes read as weaker craft if applied mechanically. The user's brief and the story's needs win.

Entries marked **[Tier A]** apply to every piece. Everything else is a lever: pick a few that suit the premise (see SKILL.md).

## 1. Meaning and theme

The largest cluster of human/AI gaps. AI fiction shows more of this than human fiction, so treat the human level as the ceiling.

**R1 — Thematic explicitness [Tier A].** Evidence: AI stories rate as more thematically explicit.
Don't articulate the theme or moral: not in narration, not in a character's summarizing line, not in the final paragraph. Theme should be inferable from event and image.
Skip when: a character states a belief as *characterization* (a zealot's speech, a parent's pet maxim) and the story does not endorse it.

**R2 — Narratorial thematic commentary [Tier A].** Evidence: AI stories contain narrator commentary on their theme much more often.
The narrator doesn't comment on the story's meaning beyond what a character could perceive. No sentence exists to tell the reader what the story is about.
Skip when: the narrator is a character with a personality (first-person, essayistic, unreliable). Commentary in that voice is allowed if it's the narrator's take rather than the story's verdict.

**R3 — Moral or philosophical weighting [Tier A].** Evidence: AI stories are more organized around moral or philosophical questions.
Organize the story around a concrete want, a concrete obstacle, and a concrete consequence. The philosophy is a residue, not the frame.

**R4 — Thematic unity (lever).** Evidence: AI stories are more perfectly unified around a theme.
Include at least one image, scene, digression, or detail that is present because it happened, not because it means something. Perfect tidiness is a tell.
Skip when: micro pieces, where every detail earns its place.

**R5 — Dialogue function [Tier A].** Evidence: AI stories more often contain philosophical debate in dialogue.
Dialogue advances plot, reveals character, evades, negotiates, misunderstands. No character debates the theme. No mentor speech, no thesis exchange, no confrontation where both parties articulate their positions clearly.

**R6 — Ending discipline [Tier A].**
The paper's canonical AI failure: a grieving character's arc ends with the narrator stating the lesson. The final paragraph should not contain a summarizing reflection, a lesson, an image whose only job is closure, or a restatement of the opening. End on action, speech, or an unexplained concrete detail. No epilogue or years-later coda that settles fates.
Note: a flash-forward *earlier* in the piece (R24) is a different move. It withholds; a terminal time-jump settles.

## 2. Sensory and emotional handling

**R7 — Emotional expression [Tier A, with a craft caveat].** Evidence: AI stories render emotion through the body far more often, and name emotions plainly far less often. This was a large gap.
Don't default to staging emotion through the body. Where a beat can be served by plain naming ("she was afraid," "he felt nothing at all") or by action and speech, use those. Per piece: at least one significant emotional beat is named plainly or carried by action, and no beat leans on the stock body-sensation list.
Stock list to avoid: tightening chest, tight throat, throat closing, cold sweat, clenched jaw, held breath, breath catching, stomach dropping/knotting/hollowing, heart hammering/pounding/stuttering, prickling skin, buzzing hands, ice in the veins, something loosening or unclenching in the chest. Don't invent fresh variants of the same device; the problem is the strategy, not the phrasing.
Craft caveat: naming emotions is not automatically better. Overused, it reads flat. The point is that AI fiction reaches for the body almost reflexively, so make it a decision. A single plainly named emotion among concrete action often lands harder than a paragraph of physiological description. Skip when: the user's genre or voice calls for embodied writing (some horror, some romance).

**R8 — Sensory density [Tier A].** Evidence: AI stories use denser sensory description.
Keep sensory description to what carries weight. Most rooms need one detail or none. No sense-sweep on entering a space.

**R9 — Smell [Tier A].** Evidence: AI stories lean on smell more often.
Use smell only when it does real work, and rarely: about once per piece at most. No "smelled of rain and diesel," no "the air smelled of," no smell-triad openers. Prefer sight and sound; leave many scenes sensorily unremarked.

**R10 — Setting as psychological mirror [Tier A by default; lever otherwise].** Evidence: AI stories mirror the protagonist's inner state in the setting more.
Weather, light, and architecture shouldn't track the protagonist's inner state. As a lever: give one scene a setting that is indifferent or tonally wrong for what happens in it (a devastating conversation in pleasant sunshine, a triumph in tedious drizzle). No dimming lamps as fear rises.

**R11 — Environmental emphasis (lever).** Evidence: AI stories give the natural environment more thematic emphasis.
Don't give nature or ecology a thematic presence by default; no landscape passages doing thematic work.
Skip when: the premise is about place or ecology.

**R12 — Depth of interior access (lever).** Evidence: AI stories give deeper access to characters' interiors.
Reduce interiority. Let characters sometimes act without their reasoning narrated, and leave at least one significant decision unexplained from the inside.

**R13 — Spatial granularity and opening grounding (lever).** Evidence: AI stories establish space more precisely and ground their openings more in a located setting.
Don't establish physical space more precisely than the story needs, and consider not grounding the opening in a located, furnished setting. Open mid-speech, mid-thought, mid-argument, on a fact, or on a line of dialogue. Rooms can stay geometrically vague.

## 3. Causality and shape

**R14 — Continuity of the main causal chain (lever).** Evidence: AI stories have a more continuous, tightly linked causal chain.
Let the chain bend. Include at least one significant event whose cause is not the inciting incident: coincidence, someone else's unrelated agenda, weather, bureaucracy, bad luck. Leave a thread unresolved and unacknowledged.
Skip when: tight genre forms (a locked-room mystery) that depend on causal closure.

**R15 — Subplot (lever, medium and long pieces).** Evidence: AI stories are more often single-track; human stories more often carry a thematically parallel subplot.
A story with a single track is the AI default. In pieces long enough to hold one, add a subplot that runs parallel to or against the main line, not independent filler and not a neat mirror that resolves in sync with the main plot.
Skip when: under ~2,000 words.

**R16 — Agency in resolution (lever).** Evidence: AI stories more often resolve through the protagonist's own choice.
Let something other than the protagonist's decision carry real weight in how things resolve: an external event, another character's choice, an institution, an accident, or nothing resolving. The protagonist is often not the most consequential actor in their own story.

**R17 — Mode of resolution (lever).** Evidence: AI stories more often end in internal understanding.
Avoid the ending where the protagonist reaches acceptance, insight, or peace. Resolve externally, or leave it unresolved.

**R18 — Moral polarity toward the protagonist (lever).** Evidence: human stories more often leave the protagonist morally ambivalent.
Make the central choice genuinely defensible and genuinely damaging, and let the narrative decline to adjudicate. Avoid the protagonist whose choices are clearly right and the redemption arc that lands.

**R19 — Pre-threat character investment (lever).** Evidence: AI stories build more sympathy before introducing threat.
Don't always build sympathy before jeopardy. Cut the warm establishing sequence; the threat can arrive before the reader has been taught to care.

**R20 — Character introduction (lever).** Evidence: AI stories introduce characters through external description far more often. Introducing them in dialogue was a distinctive human habit.
Don't introduce the central character by describing them. Lead with dialogue (mid-conversation, before the reader knows who is speaking) or another character's report of them. An unexplained action is a rarer third option: used as a standing formula it becomes its own fingerprint (see C10, Kimi), so vary the method between pieces. Avoid appearance, age, profession, or a summarizing trait sentence as the introduction.

**R21 — Multiple protagonists (lever, longer pieces).**
A single main character is not the default. Consider two or more genuine main characters with independent claims on the narrative.
Compatibility note: this is about *who the story is about*, not *whose head we're in*. Two protagonists can be carried by one focalizing consciousness (R34). If you rotate focalization, do it visibly and deliberately.
Skip when: under ~3,000 words.

**R22 — Social network expansion (lever).**
The protagonist's social world need not grow over the story. It can contract: people leave, relationships end and are not replaced, and the protagonist finishes more alone than they began, with no compensating community.

**R42 — Event escalation and event-type diversity (lever).**
Don't write flat, evenly modulated event intensity. Escalate unevenly: quiet stretches, a sharp jump, a plateau. Vary event *type*, so the story isn't built from one kind of beat (conversations only, or realizations only). Micro pieces: two event types are enough.

## 4. Time

Temporal structure was a weaker signal on its own than some other dimensions, but models tend to be alike in how they handle time. Treat these as high-value levers for medium and long pieces.

**R23 — Chronological discontinuity (lever).** Evidence: human stories are less chronological than AI stories.
Straight first-to-last telling is the AI default. Consider two or more time jumps not signposted with crutch transitions ("Three years earlier," "Later that evening").
Skip when: micro pieces; real-time scenes.

**R24 — Anachrony intensity (lever).** Evidence: human stories use flashbacks and flash-forwards more.
Use flashbacks and flash-forwards. Include a flash-forward that shows an outcome before its cause. Place it mid-story, where it withholds. A terminal flash-forward that settles fates is the epilogue R6 rules out.

**R25 — Nonlinear framing for delayed disclosure (lever).** Evidence: human stories use nonlinear framing more to delay disclosure.
Use time structure to withhold. In the paper's phrasing, a human mystery might open at the funeral and spiral backward through decades, where an AI tells it from first clue to grand reveal.

**R26 — Recontextualization after surprise (lever).** Evidence: human stories more often recontextualize earlier events after a surprise.
A revelation should force rereading: it changes what earlier scenes meant, not just adds information. Plant the material that will be reread.

**R27 — Revelation pacing (lever).**
Don't front-load crucial context. Withhold, and let the reader see that something is being withheld.

## 5. Reader relationship and world

**R28 — Named real-world references (lever).** Evidence: human stories name real-world references much more often.
AI tends to avoid naming real brands, places, and works. Where it suits the piece, name real things: specific authors, titles, songs, brands, streets, cities, institutions, historical events. About two per piece of medium length.
Caution: only name things you are confident are accurate. A wrong street or a fabricated "real" fact is worse than vagueness. Skip when: the setting is invented and naming would break it.

**R29 — Reference explicitness (lever).** Evidence: human stories mix explicit and implicit references more; AI stories lean on implicit echoes alone.
Don't let diffuse allusion be the only mode. Mix named references with implicit echoes.

**R30 — Fourth-wall permeability (lever, conditional).** Evidence: human stories more often acknowledge their own telling and address the reader directly.
AI fiction tends to be written as though no one is watching. Let the telling show itself once, lightly: a narrator admitting they are selecting what to tell, an aside, an admitted gap in knowledge, a note that a scene is reconstructed.
Decide by the piece, not by a rate. Use it when the voice invites it (first person, a narrator with a personality, a story about testimony or memory). Skip it in tight close-third or immersive real-time scenes. Direct address to the reader is rarer and should be a genuine aside, not decorative "dear reader."

**R31 — Genre crossover (lever).**
Consider not writing cleanly inside one genre: domestic realism with procedural, ghost story with office comedy, historical with speculative.

**R32 — Location variety (lever, medium and long).** Evidence: human stories use more distinct locations.
Use more distinct locations than the plot strictly requires, including one visited once and never returned to.
Skip when: short pieces and single-setting forms.

**R33 — Dialogue-to-narration proportion (lever).** Evidence: human stories have more dialogue relative to narration.
Increase dialogue relative to narration where it suits; let scenes run as talk, including talk that goes nowhere.

**R34 — Focalization (lever).**
Prefer one focalizing consciousness sustained throughout over rotating access to several characters' interiors. If you rotate, do it deliberately (see R21).

## 6. Rarity and originality

**R35 — Rare combinations (lever).** Evidence: human stories sit in rarer regions of the paper's combined feature space, and vary more from one another than AI stories do.
After the other choices, make at least two choices that are unusual *in combination*: an uncommon pairing of point of view, tense, form, timescale, genre register, or structural device. Don't settle on the most defensible version of each decision independently. Also, two pieces written from similar prompts shouldn't resemble each other in structure.

**R36 — Canonical text [Tier A].**
Never reproduce or closely paraphrase canonical public-domain stories. The paper's memorization audit concentrates risk in *The Yellow Wallpaper*, *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow*, *Heart of Darkness*, *The Call of Cthulhu*, and stories by Poe, Doyle, Wilde, and Dickens. Naming them (R28) is fine; imitating their text is not.

## 7. Length

**R43 — Word counts [Tier A].**
When a target length is given, land within ±10%. The paper found that models often miss requested lengths, some undershooting and some overshooting. Count and adjust before delivering. Models count words unreliably by eye, so if a code tool is available, use it to count the draft. Don't stop at a natural-feeling ending short of the target, and don't pad past it.
